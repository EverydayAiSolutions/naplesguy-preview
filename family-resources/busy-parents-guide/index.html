Busy Parents Guide:
raising kids when both parents work

Simple, sourced ideas for weekday routines, sharing the load, making time as a couple, and teaching real-life lessons — plus official help in Naples and Collier County.

Jeff Silberman, The Naples Guy, put this guide together for families who are doing a lot — two jobs, kids, and a house that still needs to run. It is not a paid program or a complete system. It gathers practical ideas from pediatric, child-development, and family-research sources, plus official local links, so busy parents can start with something useful and check the details themselves.

Every family is different. This page is general information, not medical, mental-health, or legal advice. Phone numbers, fees, and program details can change, so families should confirm them on the official pages before they go.

Quick answers

A short version of the guide, for parents who only have a minute.

What helps most when both parents work?

A weekday rhythm that stays mostly the same, a fair split of the work at home, and small daily moments of real attention. Perfect days are not the goal. A routine the family can repeat usually is.

How can parents stay close to kids on a busy day?

Short moments count. A few minutes with the phone put away, a talk in the car, cooking side by side, or a book at bedtime can keep a child feeling noticed.

How can a couple make time for each other?

Plan a regular date, even if it is an hour at home after bedtime, and try a short daily check-in. Treat that time like an appointment, not a leftover.

When should kids start helping at home?

Preschoolers can already help with simple jobs, such as tidying toys or putting clothes in a basket. Tasks should grow with the child’s age, and effort matters more than a perfect result.

What if parents feel guilty or worn out?

Those feelings are common in homes where both parents work. It can help to lower the bar to a good-enough day, share the load more evenly, and ask for help if the stress does not ease.

The weekday

Routines that work when both parents work

Children feel safer when the day is predictable. Parents often feel calmer too, because fewer decisions have to be made in the rush. The outline below is a starting point, not a schedule to copy hour by hour.

Mornings

Mornings go more smoothly when many decisions are made the night before: clothes, backpacks, lunches, and a simple breakfast plan. A consistent wake-up, get-dressed, eat, and leave order helps children know what comes next. Giving a preschooler a couple of simple choices, such as which shirt to wear, can cut down on battles without handing over the whole morning.

Source: Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, Tips for Building Structure. Source: CDC, Positive Parenting Tips for preschoolers.

After school

The first hour after school often sets the tone for the evening. A snack, a little movement, and a short decompression period before homework can help. If after-school care is part of the week, ask how homework and play are handled there, then keep the evening plan simple when everyone gets home.

Source: Child Mind Institute, guidance on bringing structure back into children’s days. Official after-school details for Collier County Public Schools are in the Naples help section below.

Dinner

Eating together, with screens off, gives families a natural time to talk and a little more control over what is served. It does not have to be every night, or a complicated meal. If dinner is messy or late, connection can still happen over breakfast, a snack at the table, or a talk in the car.

Source: Mayo Clinic, “Make time for family meals.” Source: Child Mind Institute, “Family Dinner: Does It Matter?”

Bedtime

A short, repeatable wind-down — pajamas, teeth, a book, lights out — helps children settle. Talk through each step so a child knows what is next. The American Academy of Pediatrics endorses these sleep ranges in a 24-hour period: 10 to 13 hours, including naps, for ages 3 to 5; 9 to 12 hours for ages 6 to 12; and 8 to 10 hours for ages 13 to 18. Keep phones, tablets, and televisions out of bedrooms when possible.

Source: ZERO TO THREE, “The Practical Magic of Daily Routines.” Source: American Academy of Pediatrics endorsement of recommended sleep times. Source: CDC, Positive Parenting Tips for preschoolers.

Screen time

For children 2 to 5, pediatric guidance still points toward about an hour a day of high-quality programming, watched with a parent when possible. For school-age children and teens, there is no single number that fits every child. The more useful test is whether screens are crowding out sleep, outdoor play, homework, reading, and time with people. A written family media plan, screen-free meals, and devices charged outside bedrooms make the rules easier to keep.

Source: American Academy of Pediatrics and HealthyChildren.org, Family Media Plan and Kids & Screen Time guidance. Source: CDC, screen-time notes for preschoolers.

Two jobs, one house

Sharing the load

When both parents work, the household still has a long list: meals, laundry, appointments, homework, and the mental work of remembering it all. Naming who does what, and revisiting that plan, usually works better than hoping it evens out on its own.

A common arrangement, and an uneven split

Pew Research Center’s analysis of U.S. Census Bureau data found that, as of 2025, 52 percent of different-sex married or cohabiting couples with children under 18 had two parents working full time. That share was 46 percent a decade earlier and 31 percent in 1975.

In Pew’s 2026 survey of working parents, even in homes where both parents worked full time, 52 percent said the mother does more of the day-to-day parenting, 39 percent said it is about equal, and 10 percent said the father does more. For household chores, 43 percent said the mother does more, 40 percent said it is about equal, and 17 percent said the father does more. Mothers and fathers often described the same household differently.

Source: Pew Research Center, “How family work arrangements have changed over time,” and “How working parents divide family responsibilities,” June 16, 2026.

A fairer week, in plain terms

These figures are not a judgment on any one family. They are a useful prompt to look at the actual week. A shared calendar, standing jobs (who owns breakfast, who owns bedtime), and a short Sunday check-in can keep one person from carrying all of the invisible work — the forms, the gifts, the doctor appointments, the mental list.

The American Academy of Pediatrics also notes that chores go more smoothly when they are part of a regular routine, when expectations are clear, and when parents praise effort instead of nagging. The same ideas help adults share the load with each other.

Source: American Academy of Pediatrics / HealthyChildren.org, “Age-Appropriate Chores for Children.”

Connection in small doses

Connecting with kids in ordinary moments

Busy parents often wait for a long stretch of free time that does not arrive. Child-development research points to something more realistic: many short, responsive exchanges during the day you already have.

Back-and-forth, in seconds

The Center on the Developing Child at Harvard University describes “serve and return”: a child reaches out with a sound, a look, or a question, and an adult notices and responds. Those turns, a bit like a simple game of catch, help build the architecture of a child’s brain and the relationship under it.

This does not require a special toy or a free hour. It can happen while buckling a seat belt, making dinner, walking into school, or getting ready in the morning. Notice what the child is focused on, name it, take turns, and follow their cue when they are ready to stop or start something new.

Source: Center on the Developing Child at Harvard University, Serve and Return, and “5 Steps for Brain-Building Serve and Return.”

Daily attention, not only dinner

Shared meals are a good habit, but they are not the only way to stay close. The Child Mind Institute has pointed out that other ordinary times — especially car rides — are often when children, including teenagers, talk about their lives. What matters is the habit of paying attention and expecting to know how a child’s day really went.

A few minutes of undivided attention, with the phone put away, can be enough to start. So can cooking together, a short walk, or reading one book at bedtime. ZERO TO THREE also notes that talking a child through the steps of a familiar routine helps them feel safe and included.

Source: Child Mind Institute, “Family Dinner: Does It Matter?” Source: ZERO TO THREE, “The Practical Magic of Daily Routines.”

The relationship under the family

Making time for each other

When the calendar is full, the couple relationship is often what gets leftover time — or none at all. The Gottman Institute’s guidance for busy couples is straightforward: protect a little time on purpose, and use ordinary moments to stay interested in each other’s days.

A weekly date, even at home

The Gottman Institute describes a date as a planned time to leave work talk behind and focus on each other. It does not have to be expensive or long. An hour can be enough. The important part is to put it on the calendar and treat it as a “no matter what” appointment.

If childcare is hard to arrange, an at-home date after the children are asleep still counts: cook something simple, play a game, take a walk around the block, or sit on the couch and talk with the television off. Trading childcare with another family, or asking a trusted relative, can make an outing possible some weeks.

Source: The Gottman Institute, “If You’re Too Busy for Date Night, You’re Too Busy,” and related guidance on couple time for parents.

A short daily check-in

Gottman writers also suggest a brief daily ritual of connection — about 20 minutes to talk and listen, without trying to fix every problem in the moment. Some couples do this after dinner, before the last of the cleanup, while they still have a little energy. A six-second kiss at the door, a mid-day text to check in, or a kind word about something the other person did well are small, repeatable habits.

One Gottman summary of relationship time also includes a longer weekly date, on the order of two hours when that is possible, used for open-ended questions about each other’s week, stresses, and hopes.

Source: The Gottman Institute, “The Busy Couple’s Guide to Sharing Fondness and Admiration,” and “6 Hours a Week to a Better Relationship.”

Simple Naples date ideas

A date here does not have to be a big night out. A walk on a public beach, a picnic at a county park, or a quiet hour after drop-off can be enough. Confirm parking, hours, and fees on the official pages before you go.

Skills that travel

Real-life lessons and chores by age

Helping at home teaches skills a child will need later: cooking, cleaning, managing money, and being part of a team. Pediatric sources say it is better to start with a few jobs that fit the child’s age, praise the effort, and add more over time, than to wait for a child to be “less busy.”

Ages 3 to 5 Ages 6 to 8 Ages 9 to 12 Teens

Ages 3 to 5

Preschoolers often want to help. Short jobs, done together, teach that they have a real place in the family.

Chores and responsibility

  • Help put toys away after play.
  • Put dirty clothes in a hamper or help load the washer.
  • Help set the table or clear a few items, with supervision.
  • Help fill a pet’s water bowl or water a plant.
  • Help with simple meal steps, such as tearing lettuce or stirring, while an adult stays close.

Money, cooking, and everyday skills

  • At this age, a sticker chart or a clear “job done” mark is often a better teaching tool than cash.
  • Let the child help with grocery trips by finding a familiar item or placing produce in a bag.
  • Offer two simple choices when you can, such as which fruit to pack, so the child practices deciding.

Values and connection

  • Play with other children, and talk about taking turns and being a friend.
  • Read together every day when you can, and visit the library.
  • When a child is upset, help them name the feeling and take the next small step, rather than solving it all at once.

Preschoolers generally need 10 to 13 hours of sleep in a 24-hour period, including naps. Source: CDC Positive Parenting Tips for preschoolers; Raising Children Network, household chores; American Academy of Pediatrics, chores and money habits; AAP-endorsed sleep times.

Ages 6 to 8

Early school years are a good time to make a few jobs part of the daily rhythm, at the same time each day.

Chores and responsibility

  • Make the bed and put dirty clothes in the hamper.
  • Set or clear the table, and put toys or books back where they belong.
  • Help sort laundry, sweep a room, or empty a small wastebasket.
  • Feed a pet or fill a water bowl.
  • Help pack a lunch, with an adult checking what goes in.

Money, cooking, and everyday skills

  • If the family uses an allowance, begin a simple save, spend, and share split, using jars or envelopes the child can see.
  • Let the child help pack a snack or do a supervised step in dinner, such as washing produce or stirring a pot.
  • Introduce one new job at a time if chores are new, so the list does not feel like too much.

Values and connection

  • Praise the effort, not a perfect result.
  • At dinner or bedtime, try a short habit of naming one good thing from the day, or one person who was kind.
  • Keep a little one-on-one time in the week, even if it is a walk or a book.

Children ages 6 to 12 generally need 9 to 12 hours of sleep. Source: American Academy of Pediatrics, age-appropriate chores and money habits; Raising Children Network; Greater Good Science Center on small gratitude habits; AAP-endorsed sleep times.

Ages 9 to 12

Older children can take on jobs that really help the household, and they can start to manage more of their own things.

Chores and responsibility

  • Vacuum, put away groceries, and put away their own laundry.
  • Help make dinner and prepare a simple snack.
  • Walk a pet, if it is safe to do so in the neighborhood.
  • By about 11 or 12, many children can, with teaching, unload a dishwasher, change sheets, fold laundry, or cook a simple meal while an adult is present.
  • Watching a younger sibling, if a family chooses that, should happen only with an adult at home.

Money, cooking, and everyday skills

  • Let the child manage a regular amount of money and make some of their own spending choices, including small mistakes that are safe to make.
  • Agree on a dollar amount that needs a parent’s OK before a purchase.
  • Invite the child to help plan a simple meal, shop from a short list, and cook it with supervision.

Values and connection

  • Ask the child to help choose a cause or a person to share with, if the family uses a “share” portion of money.
  • Keep talking in ordinary moments — car rides, a walk, or time in the kitchen — and listen more than you lecture.
  • Devices are a privilege. They should not replace sleep, homework, outdoor time, or family meals.

This age still falls in the 9-to-12-hour sleep range. Source: American Academy of Pediatrics, chores and money habits; Raising Children Network; Child Mind Institute on staying connected; AAP-endorsed sleep times.

Teens

Teenagers can run many household jobs on their own. The goal is skill and reliability, not a long chore list for its own sake.

Chores and responsibility

  • Do their own laundry and help keep shared spaces clean, including a bathroom.
  • Cook a simple family meal on a regular night, or take a standing dinner job.
  • Do basic grocery shopping from a list, yard work, or other larger jobs the family needs.
  • If a teen drives, help with agreed-upon errands.

Money, cooking, and everyday skills

  • Keep any allowance or earnings predictable, and let teens budget for some of their own wants so they feel the trade-offs.
  • For a bigger, reasonable purchase, set a savings goal. Some families match a portion of what the teen earns.
  • Practice real kitchen skills: a protein, a vegetable, and a staple they can cook without a recipe card every time.

Values and connection

  • Stay in the habit of knowing a teen’s friends, plans, and worries, even when they seem to want more space.
  • There is no single “right” amount of screen time for teens. Watch whether it is getting in the way of sleep, school, activity, and close relationships.
  • Keep some device-free times, including meals and the hour before bed, and keep the conversation about online life open.

Teens generally need 8 to 10 hours of sleep. Source: Raising Children Network; American Academy of Pediatrics, money habits and media guidance; Child Mind Institute; AAP-endorsed sleep times.

A simple way to teach money

The American Academy of Pediatrics suggests teaching children to split money into three uses: save, spend, and share. Save is for a goal you agree on. Spend is for the child’s own choices, within family rules. Share is for giving to a person or cause the child helps pick. Jars, envelopes, or a simple notebook all work. Families can decide whether an allowance is tied to chores or is separate; what matters is that the plan is clear and consistent.

Source: American Academy of Pediatrics / HealthyChildren.org, “Kids & Money: Help Your Child Learn Good Financial Habits.”

A kind look at a hard feeling

Handling guilt and stress

Many working parents worry that they are not doing enough at home, at work, or both. That worry is understandable. It does not mean a family is failing.

What the research shows, and what it does not

In Pew’s 2026 survey, most parents in dual full-time households said the arrangement helped their family’s finances (83 percent). Views about children’s well-being were more mixed: 49 percent saw a positive effect, 22 percent a negative one, and 29 percent neither. Those are feelings, not a grade on any child. Plenty of families with two working parents raise children who are close, capable, and well cared for.

Source: Pew Research Center, “How family work arrangements have changed over time,” June 16, 2026.

When tiredness turns into something heavier

The American Psychological Association has reported on parental burnout: a deep exhaustion in the parenting role, a sense of being fed up, and, in some cases, pulling away emotionally. Researchers note that any parent can experience it, not only parents in unusual circumstances. Helpful steps from psychologists quoted in that reporting include lowering perfectionistic “shoulds,” taking small breaks, showing yourself the same compassion you would offer a friend, and planning a small, low-stakes moment with your children that reminds you why the work matters.

You can love your children and still find parenting hard. If sadness, anxiety, or exhaustion are not easing, a pediatrician, a qualified mental-health professional, or 211 is a better next step than a webpage. This section is information, not a diagnosis or a treatment plan.

Source: American Psychological Association, “The impact of parental burnout,” October 2021.

A week at a glance

A simple checklist

Use this as a reminder, not a scorecard. Print it if that helps, and skip anything that does not fit your family this week.

Busy parents’ weekly reminders

  • Lay out clothes, backpacks, and a breakfast plan the night before.
  • Keep the morning in a simple, repeatable order.
  • Plan the first hour after school: snack, movement, then homework or downtime.
  • Aim for at least a few device-free meals or snacks together this week.
  • Give each child a few minutes of undivided attention.
  • Look for small back-and-forth moments during ordinary tasks.
  • Check that household jobs and the “thinking work” of the week are shared fairly.
  • Schedule a weekly time for the couple, even if it is at home after bedtime.
  • Try a short daily check-in with each other, phones put away.
  • Give each child one or two regular chores that fit their age.
  • Keep the bedtime routine short and the same most nights.
  • Charge devices outside bedrooms, and keep screens out of meals.
  • Name one good thing from the day at dinner or bedtime.
  • Look at one official Naples resource this month — library, parks, or after-school care.
  • If stress or sadness is not easing, ask for help (a pediatrician, a trusted friend, or 211).
Questions

Questions parents often ask

These answers come from the sources linked in this guide. Details can change, so families should confirm local programs on the official pages.

Is this a paid parenting program?

No. This is a free guide on The Naples Guy website. It gathers practical ideas from pediatric, child-development, and family-research sources, plus official links for families in Naples and Collier County. It is not a complete system, and it is not medical, mental-health, or legal advice. Families should use what fits and confirm local details on the official pages.

Do both parents in the United States usually work full time?

Pew Research Center’s analysis of U.S. Census Bureau data found that, as of 2025, 52 percent of different-sex married or cohabiting couples with children under 18 had two parents working full time, up from 46 percent a decade earlier and 31 percent in 1975. Not every family looks like that, including single-parent households. The point is that two working parents is now a common arrangement, and the household load still needs a clear plan. Source: Pew Research Center, June 16, 2026, reports on family work arrangements.

How much sleep do children need?

The American Academy of Pediatrics has endorsed these ranges for a 24-hour period: 10 to 13 hours, including naps, for ages 3 to 5; 9 to 12 hours for ages 6 to 12; and 8 to 10 hours for ages 13 to 18. A consistent bedtime routine and keeping screens out of the bedroom can help children get that rest. Source: American Academy of Pediatrics endorsement of recommended sleep times.

How much screen time is recommended?

For preschoolers, CDC and pediatric guidance still points toward limiting high-quality screen time to about one hour a day and watching together when possible. For school-age children and teens, the American Academy of Pediatrics no longer gives one daily number for every child. Families are encouraged to make a Family Media Plan and to keep screens from crowding out sleep, play, homework, reading, and time with people. Source: CDC Positive Parenting Tips for preschoolers. Source: American Academy of Pediatrics Family Media Plan.

Does a family have to eat dinner together every night?

No. Shared meals are a useful habit, and turning screens off at the table helps. Research and clinical writers also note that staying in touch in other ordinary moments — including car rides — can matter as much as a formal dinner. A few meals together in a week is a solid start. Source: Mayo Clinic, “Make time for family meals.” Source: Child Mind Institute, “Family Dinner: Does It Matter?”

What before- and after-school care does Collier County Public Schools offer?

Collier County Public Schools runs a School Age Childcare (SACC) program before and after school at listed elementary campuses. Published hours on regular school days are 6:30 to 7:45 a.m. before school and 2:50 to 6:30 p.m. after school. Fees, school sites, and calendars can change, so families should confirm them on the official SACC page. Some elementary schools use other listed providers, including After School Programs, Inc., Sports Club of Naples, and the Marco YMCA, which are named on that same official page. Source: Collier County Public Schools, School Age Childcare.

Where can a Naples family get help if things feel too heavy?

If someone is in danger, call 911. For local, non-emergency help with food, housing, health programs, and other services, United Way of Collier and the Keys operates 211. Families can dial 211 or 239-263-4211, or text a ZIP code to 898211. A pediatrician is also a good first call when a child’s behavior, sleep, or mood is a concern. This guide is not a substitute for professional care. Source: United Way of Collier and the Keys, 211.

What is Healthy Start in Collier County?

The Florida Department of Health in Collier County describes Healthy Start as a free home-visiting program that offers education and care coordination to pregnant women and families of children under age 3. Services include prenatal and parenting education, help finding community resources, and stress-management education. Families can call 239-252-5317 to ask about an appointment. Source: Florida Department of Health in Collier County, Healthy Start.
