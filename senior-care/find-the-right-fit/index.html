Find the Best Plan

Which kind of care fits, in plain language.

How do you know which kind of care is right?

Most families end up here after something changes. A fall, a hospital stay, a missed medication, or just a feeling that Mom or Dad isn’t doing as well on their own. You don’t have to figure it all out at once. Start with three questions:

  • SafetyIs Mom or Dad safe at home alone right now, day and night?
  • HelpDo they need hands-on help, like bathing, dressing or medications, or mostly help with the house, meals and rides?
  • MemoryIs memory the main worry, like getting lost, repeating questions or leaving the stove on?

The answers usually point to one or two of the options below. Each one covers when families tend to choose it, what a normal day looks like, who pays, and the signs it may be time for more help.

Staying home, with help

Home care

Families usually start here when Mom is still safe at home but needs a hand with bathing, meals, errands or rides to the doctor.

Usually a fit when

  • She wants to stay home, and the home is still safe for her
  • She needs help a few hours a day or a few days a week
  • The family caregiver needs a break, or lives too far away to help every day

A typical day

A caregiver comes for a set shift, often four hours or more. They might help her shower and dress, make lunch, do a load of laundry, pick up a prescription and drive her to an appointment. Many families start with two or three visits a week and add more as needs grow.

Cost and who pays

Usually paid privately by the hour. In Florida, the typical rate is about $32 an hour, or roughly $6,100 a month at 44 hours a week. Medicare does not pay for this kind of everyday help on its own. Long-term care insurance may help, depending on the policy, and Florida Medicaid’s long-term care program can cover in-home help for people who qualify.

Time to look at the next step when

  • She needs someone there most of the day, or overnight
  • The hours, and the bill, keep climbing. Care around the clock at home can cost far more than assisted living
  • She is lonely and isolated between visits

Medical care at home

Home health (Medicare)

This is the one most people mix up with home care. Home health is short-term medical care at home, ordered by a doctor, usually after a hospital stay, a surgery or a flare-up of a health problem.

Usually a fit when

  • Dad just came home from the hospital and needs a nurse to check a wound or sort out new medications
  • He needs physical or occupational therapy to get his strength back
  • Leaving the house is hard for him right now

A typical day

A nurse or therapist visits for about an hour, a few times a week. They check vitals, change dressings, work on walking and balance, and show the family what to watch for. An aide may help with bathing while the skilled care is going on. Between visits, the family is in charge.

Cost and who pays

Medicare pays the full cost of covered home health visits when he is homebound, a doctor orders the care and a Medicare-certified agency provides it. It is part-time care, generally up to 28 hours a week. It does not cover care around the clock, meal delivery, or help with bathing and dressing when that is the only need. Medicare Advantage plans have their own rules, so call the plan.

Time to look at the next step when

  • The visits end but he still needs daily help. That is when families add home care
  • He is not safe at home alone between visits

Daytime company and care

Adult day programs

Adult day programs are a good fit when Mom does well with people around but shouldn’t be home alone all day, especially when a spouse or grown child is the main caregiver and still works or badly needs a break.

Usually a fit when

  • She is lonely or bored at home
  • She has some memory loss but sleeps safely at home with family there at night
  • The caregiver needs a few hours to work, run errands or rest

A typical day

Drop-off in the morning, then exercise, music, games, a hot lunch and time with friends, with staff keeping an eye on everyone. Some programs also help with medications or bathing, and some offer rides. Pick-up is in the late afternoon.

Cost and who pays

Usually paid privately by the day. In Florida, the typical rate is about $75 a day, or roughly $1,625 a month, far less than full-time home care. Medicare does not pay for adult day programs. Florida Medicaid’s long-term care program includes adult day health care for people who qualify, and some long-term care insurance policies help.

Time to look at the next step when

  • Nights become the hard part: getting up confused, wandering or falling
  • The caregiver is worn out even with the daytime break

Done with the house

Independent living

Independent living is for parents who are doing fine but are tired of the house: the yard, the upkeep, cooking every night and being alone so much.

Usually a fit when

  • They are healthy and still getting around on their own
  • They want friends, activities and dinner without the work
  • The house is too big, or riding out hurricane season alone is a worry

A typical day

Breakfast on their own schedule, a fitness class or a card game, lunch with friends, maybe a shuttle to the store or the doctor, and dinner in the dining room. Housekeeping and repairs are handled. If help is needed later, home care can come in, and many communities have assisted living on the same campus.

Cost and who pays

Private pay. Most communities charge monthly rent that covers meals, activities and upkeep, and some life-care communities also charge an entrance fee. Medicare and Medicaid do not pay for independent living, so families usually use income, savings or the sale of the house.

Time to look at the next step when

  • Missed medications or a fall
  • Trouble bathing or dressing
  • Needing reminders to eat or keep appointments

Daily help, own apartment

Assisted living

Families usually look at assisted living when Mom needs help every day with things like medications, showering or getting dressed, but doesn’t need a nurse around the clock.

Usually a fit when

  • There have been falls, or close calls, at home
  • Medications are missed or mixed up
  • She isn’t eating well, the house is slipping, or she is alone too much
  • Home care hours keep going up

A typical day

She has her own apartment, with staff on site 24 hours a day. An aide helps her shower and dress in the morning and brings medications on time. She has three meals in the dining room, activities and outings, and housekeeping and laundry are done for her. She keeps her own routine and her own things, with help a call button away.

Cost and who pays

Mostly private pay. In Florida, the typical monthly rate is about $5,610, and prices vary a lot by apartment and care level, so ask each community for current rates. Medicare does not pay for assisted living. Florida Medicaid’s long-term care program can cover assisted living services for people who qualify, though there is often a waitlist. Long-term care insurance and VA Aid and Attendance can also help.

Time to look at the next step when

  • Memory changes make her unsafe, like wandering or getting lost
  • She needs nursing care around the clock

Alzheimer’s and dementia

Memory care

Memory care is for a parent living with Alzheimer’s or another dementia when safety becomes the daily worry: wandering, getting lost, leaving the stove on or forgetting to eat.

Usually a fit when

  • She wanders or tries to leave
  • Confusion is worse late in the day
  • The family can’t keep her safe at home anymore, even with help
  • There are new personality changes or agitation

A typical day

A calm, secure setting with a steady routine. Staff trained in dementia guide her through the day with music, simple activities, walks in a safe garden and meals, with help as needed. Doors are secured, so she can move around freely without getting lost.

Cost and who pays

Usually private pay, and usually more than assisted living because of extra staff and security. Medicare does not pay for it. Florida Medicaid’s long-term care program, VA benefits and long-term care insurance may help people who qualify. Florida has no separate memory care license, so ask whether the community’s license includes Specialized Alzheimer’s Services or Extended Congregate Care.

Time to look at the next step when

  • She needs skilled nursing for medical problems
  • She is in the late stage and comfort becomes the goal. Hospice can come to her in memory care

After the hospital

Rehab and skilled nursing

This usually comes up in the hospital. Dad had a fall, a surgery or a serious illness and isn’t ready to go straight home, so a short rehab stay gets him stronger first.

Usually a fit when

  • He was admitted to the hospital as an inpatient for at least three days
  • He needs therapy or nursing care every day
  • For long-term care: he needs nursing care around the clock

A typical day

Physical and occupational therapy most days, nurses managing medications and wounds, and meals provided. The goal is to get him home, or to the next right setting, safely. The team plans the discharge with the family.

Cost and who pays

After a three-day inpatient hospital stay, Medicare can cover up to 100 days as long as he needs daily skilled care. In 2026, Medicare pays in full for days 1 to 20 after the deductible, and days 21 to 100 cost $217 a day. Time “under observation” doesn’t count toward the three days, so ask the hospital. Medicare does not pay for long-term nursing home care. In Florida, a shared room typically runs about $10,342 a month. Medicaid can cover long-term nursing home care for people who qualify.

Good to know

  • Ask early where he will go after rehab. Planning before discharge day makes it far less stressful

Comfort, and a break

Hospice and respite

Hospice is comfort care when an illness can’t be cured, and it comes to wherever Mom lives: her home, assisted living, memory care or a nursing home. Respite is a short break for the family caregiver.

Usually a fit when

  • Her doctor says life expectancy is six months or less if the illness runs its course
  • Hospital trips keep happening and the family wants the focus on comfort
  • The caregiver is exhausted and needs a few days off

A typical day

A hospice team of a nurse, an aide, a social worker and a chaplain visits on a schedule and is on call around the clock. They manage pain and symptoms, bring supplies and equipment, and support the whole family. A respite stay lets a caregiver rest for a few days while someone else provides the care.

Cost and who pays

Medicare Part A pays for hospice from a Medicare-approved provider, with only small copays for some medications. It does not pay room and board in a facility. For someone on hospice, Medicare also covers short respite stays of up to five days at a time. Other respite is mostly private pay, though Medicaid and some long-term care insurance policies can help.

Costs are typical Florida rates from a national cost-of-care survey and change every year, so ask each provider for current prices. This is general information, not medical or financial advice. For more on money, see Paying for Care.
