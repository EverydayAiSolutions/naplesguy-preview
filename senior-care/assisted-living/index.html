01What assisted living is

Quick answer

  • A licensed assisted living facility provides housing, meals, and one or more personal services for more than 24 hours. Florida Statutes s. 429.02.

02Who qualifies for assisted living

Florida’s official consumer guide says assisted living is for adults who can no longer live independently and need help or supervision with daily activities or medications, or who need routine, simple nursing or mental health services. It is not the right setting if someone needs 24-hour nursing care.

The National Institute on Aging suggests spending time in the home and watching for signs such as trouble managing medications, changes around the house, significant weight change, mood changes, or memory and decision-making problems that go beyond ordinary forgetfulness. The best starting point is to ask your parent directly, then compare those signs with Florida’s picture of who is appropriate for assisted living.

Source: Florida AHCA. Source: Florida Statutes s. 429.26. Source: National Institute on Aging.

03What help is included

Florida AHCA describes typical basic services such as housing, meals (including special diets when the facility offers them), personal care, help with medications, supervision, arranging health care, transportation to health care, health monitoring, and social activities. A facility may also charge extra for additional services. The resident contract has to spell out what is included and the rates or charges.

Nursing in assisted living is limited. Facilities with a standard license may employ or contract with a nurse to give medications and perform certain other tasks, and specialty licenses can allow additional nursing. A resident may often contract with a licensed home health agency if they do not need 24-hour nursing, and they must still meet the facility’s residency rules.

Source: Florida AHCA. Source: Florida Statutes s. 429.24. Source: Florida Statutes s. 429.07.

04Assisted living compared with independent living and nursing home care

Assisted living is for people who need help with daily care but do not need the level of medical care in a nursing home. Independent living is generally for people who can manage on their own. Nursing homes focus more on medical care than most assisted living communities, and typically include nursing care, 24-hour supervision, meals, and help with everyday activities, with rehabilitation services also available.

05Florida licenses, not one numbered level

Florida does not use a single numbered “level of care” for every community. AHCA issues licenses in categories such as standard, extended congregate care (ECC), limited nursing services, limited mental health, and memory care services. A standard license covers personal services such as housing, meals, and help with daily activities. An ECC license is meant to help residents age in place with more support. Ask which license a community holds and what that means for the help they can give.

Florida law makes the owner or administrator responsible for deciding whether someone is appropriate for that facility, using a medical examination. Each resident must be examined by a licensed physician, physician assistant, or advanced practice registered nurse within 60 days before admission or within 30 days after admission.

Source: Florida Statutes s. 429.07. Source: Florida Statutes s. 429.26. Source: Florida AHCA.

06A short checklist

A short checklist
  • Ask which AHCA license the community holds, and look it up on FloridaHealthFinder.
  • Ask what is included in the monthly fee and what costs extra.
  • Ask about the medical examination and how the community decides whether someone is a fit.
  • Compare assisted living with independent living, memory care, and nursing home care if those are also on the table.
Good to know

Help with medications is a basic assisted living service in Florida, and residents have a right to assistance with medication management. Pets, special diets, and private versus shared rooms vary by community, so ask and get the answer in writing before you sign.

Source: Florida AHCA. Source: Florida Statutes s. 429.28.

09Before the tour

A license from AHCA is required to operate an assisted living facility in Florida. Look the community up on FloridaHealthFinder and ask which license it holds — for example a standard license, extended congregate care, limited nursing services, or memory care services. Florida law makes the owner or administrator responsible for deciding whether someone is appropriate for that facility, using a medical examination. Each resident must be examined by a licensed physician, physician assistant, or advanced practice registered nurse within 60 days before admission or within 30 days after admission.

Write down the person’s needs, including medications, mobility, memory, and any special diet, so you can ask whether the community can actually meet them. Bring the Questions to Ask list. If payment may involve Medicaid, veterans benefits, long-term care insurance, or Optional State Supplementation, ask in advance whether the community accepts them.

What this means for you. Do the license lookup at home, and go in with the person’s actual needs written down. That makes the tour a comparison, not a first impression only.

Source: Florida AHCA. Source: FloridaHealthFinder. Source: Florida Statutes s. 429.07. Source: Florida Statutes s. 429.26.

10During the tour

The National Institute on Aging suggests asking about costs and special needs, visiting more than once, including at mealtime, and looking for licensing, accessibility, residents who look well cared for, respectful staff, and a clean, well-maintained facility. Florida’s consumer guide also says to ask about special services, staff training, and, if memory is a concern, equipment used to help prevent wandering. Ask whether a nurse is on site, on what schedule, and what they can and cannot do. Ask for a written list of what is in the monthly fee and what costs extra, including any one-time move-in fee or deposit and how refunds work.

What this means for you. Notice the ordinary parts of the day — meals, hallways, how staff speak to residents — not only the welcome room. If you can, return at a different time.

Source: National Institute on Aging. Source: Florida AHCA. Source: Florida Statutes s. 429.24.

11After the tour

Read the contract carefully. Florida law requires it to spell out the services and accommodations included and the rates or charges, and to provide at least 30 days’ written notice of a rate increase. A new service or accommodation that was not previously charged does not require that 30-day notice. Compare what you were told on the tour with the contract and with the FloridaHealthFinder record.

If something about care or rights is wrong, you can contact the Florida Long-Term Care Ombudsman Program at 1-888-831-0404, or file a complaint with AHCA. Residents have rights under Florida law, including the right to share a room with a spouse if both live in the facility, subject to each person remaining appropriate for that community.

What this means for you. Do not treat a tour promise as final until it is in the contract. If two communities are still in play, compare the written rates and the licenses side by side.

Source: Florida Statutes s. 429.24. Source: Florida Statutes s. 429.28. Source: Florida Long-Term Care Ombudsman Program. Source: FloridaHealthFinder.

12Bringing family along, and settling in

The National Institute on Aging notes that moving an older adult to an assisted living facility can be stressful and is a big change, and that it may take some time for everyone to adjust. Official suggestions include getting to know the staff before the move, being an advocate for the person’s needs and preferences, being supportive and listening, sharing your own feelings with a social worker or mental health professional, checking in regularly after the move, and building a relationship with staff.

What this means for you. If you can, bring another family member on at least one visit so more than one person has seen the community in ordinary hours.

After a senior living move, Jeff offers weekly visits for a small fee; the exact amount is not listed on this site, so ask him when you call. See also Moving & Downsizing.

Source: National Institute on Aging.

Who can I trust?

Look up the license

A license from the Agency for Health Care Administration is required to operate an assisted living facility in Florida. 20Questions families often ask

Who qualifies for assisted living?

In Florida, assisted living is for adults who can no longer live independently and need help or supervision with daily activities or medications, or who need routine, simple nursing or mental health services. It is not the right setting if someone needs 24-hour nursing care. The facility’s owner or administrator decides whether a person can be admitted, based on a medical exam, the services the community can offer, and the limits of its license.

Source: Florida AHCA. Source: Florida Statutes s. 429.26.

How do I know if my parents need assisted living?

It is not always obvious, and a short phone call can hide how someone is really doing. The National Institute on Aging suggests spending time in the home and watching for signs such as trouble managing medications, changes around the house, significant weight change, mood changes, or memory and decision-making problems that go beyond ordinary forgetfulness. The best starting point is to ask your parent directly, then compare those signs with Florida’s picture of who is appropriate for assisted living — someone who needs help or supervision, but not 24-hour nursing.

Source: National Institute on Aging. Source: Florida AHCA.

Do they do an assessment before admission?

Yes. Florida law makes the owner or administrator responsible for deciding whether someone is appropriate for that facility, using a medical examination, the person’s needs and preferences, the services the facility can provide, and the limits of its license. Each resident must be examined by a licensed physician, physician assistant, or advanced practice registered nurse within 60 days before admission or within 30 days after admission. The examination is used to help decide whether admission — and later, continued residency — is appropriate.

Source: Florida Statutes s. 429.26.

What is the difference between assisted living and memory care?

Assisted living is a licensed residential setting that provides housing, meals, and personal help for people who need daily support but not 24-hour nursing. Memory care is specialized support for people with Alzheimer’s disease or another dementia; it may be offered inside an assisted living community, and not every assisted living community provides it. The Alzheimer’s Association notes that these services are not defined the same way everywhere, so you should ask what is actually offered, how staff are trained, and whether the area is secured. In Florida, a facility’s license can include “memory care services” as a category of care, along with a standard license and other specialty licenses.

Source: Florida AHCA. Source: Alzheimer's Association. Source: National Institute on Aging. Source: Florida Statutes s. 429.07.

What is the difference between assisted living and independent living?

Assisted living is for people who need help with daily care — such as bathing, dressing, medications, or supervision — but do not need the level of medical care in a nursing home. Independent living is generally for people who can manage on their own and want a maintenance-free home and social life, without that daily personal care. In Florida, a licensed assisted living facility is defined as a place that provides housing, meals, and one or more personal services for more than 24 hours.

Source: National Institute on Aging. Source: Florida Statutes s. 429.02.

What levels of care does assisted living provide?

Florida does not use a single numbered “level of care” for every community. Instead, AHCA issues licenses in categories such as standard, extended congregate care (ECC), limited nursing services, limited mental health, and memory care services. A standard license covers personal services such as housing, meals, and help with daily activities. An ECC license is meant to help residents age in place with more support, and a limited nursing services license allows certain nursing that is still not 24-hour nursing. Ask which license a community holds and what that means for the help they can give.

Source: Florida Statutes s. 429.07. Source: Florida AHCA.

Are nursing services provided in assisted living? Is there a nurse on staff?

It depends on the facility’s license. Nursing in assisted living is limited, and a need for 24-hour nursing generally means assisted living is no longer appropriate. Facilities with a standard license may employ or contract with a nurse to give medications and perform certain other tasks, and specialty licenses such as limited nursing services or extended congregate care allow additional nursing. Staffing and nurse availability vary, so ask whether a nurse is on site, on what schedule, and what they can and cannot do.

Source: Florida AHCA. Source: Florida Statutes s. 429.07.

Can someone with dementia stay in assisted living, or do they need memory care?

Some people with dementia can live in assisted living if the community can meet their needs and they do not require 24-hour nursing. Dementia services vary widely, and not every assisted living community offers specialized memory care. Florida’s consumer guide says to ask about special services, staff training, and equipment used to help prevent wandering. Visit and ask what the community can actually provide before you decide.

Source: Florida AHCA. Source: Alzheimer's Association.

Are there state rules for when someone must move from assisted living to memory care?

Florida does not have an automatic rule that someone must move from assisted living into a memory care unit at a certain stage of dementia. Whether a person can stay depends on whether that facility, under its license, can still meet their needs. In general, a Florida assisted living facility may not admit or keep someone who needs 24-hour nursing supervision or who is bedridden beyond short limits set in the statute (with some hospice and ECC exceptions). If needs change, ask the community what it can still provide and what the next step would be.

Source: Florida Statutes s. 429.26. Source: Florida AHCA.

Can couples with different care needs live together?

Florida law gives a resident the right to share a room with a spouse if both live in the facility. Each person is still assessed on their own, and both must remain appropriate for that community under its license and the services it can provide. If one spouse later needs more care than the facility can offer, the community may not be able to keep both people in the same setting. Ask how the community handles couples with different needs before you sign a contract.

Source: Florida Statutes s. 429.28. Source: Florida Statutes s. 429.26.

How much does assisted living cost?

There is no single price. Florida AHCA says fees are set by each provider and are not the same everywhere, and a community may charge a basic fee plus extra amounts for added services. The CareScout Cost of Care Survey 2025, collected from July through November 2025, lists a Florida median of $5,610 a month, or $67,320 a year, for an assisted living community. Local prices can be higher or lower, so ask each community for a current written rate.

Source: Florida AHCA. Source: CareScout Cost of Care Survey 2025.

What’s included in the monthly fee, and is pricing all-inclusive?

It is not always all-inclusive. Florida AHCA describes typical basic services such as housing, meals (including special diets when the facility offers them), personal care, help with medications, supervision, arranging health care, transportation to health care, health monitoring, and social activities — but a facility may also charge extra for additional services. The resident contract has to spell out the services and accommodations included and the rates or charges. Ask for a written list of what is in the monthly fee and what costs extra before you sign.

Source: Florida AHCA. Source: Florida Statutes s. 429.24.

Is there a move-in or community fee?

Florida does not set a standard move-in or community fee. Each facility sets its own charges, and the contract must list the rates or charges. Ask for a written explanation of any one-time fees, deposits, or advance payments, and how refunds work, before you sign.

Source: Florida AHCA. Source: Florida Statutes s. 429.24.

Can the monthly rate go up after move-in?

Yes. Florida law requires the contract to provide at least 30 days’ written notice of a rate increase, and AHCA’s consumer guide says the same for a monthly rate increase. A new service or accommodation that was not previously charged does not require that 30-day notice. Read the contract so you know how and when rates can change.

Source: Florida Statutes s. 429.24. Source: Florida AHCA.

Does Medicare pay for assisted living?

Medicare does not pay for assisted living. The National Institute on Aging notes that people usually pay the full cost themselves, though Medicaid may cover some aspects depending on the state, and some long-term care insurance policies may help. In Florida, AHCA says payment is usually from private funds, and you should ask whether a community accepts long-term care insurance, veterans benefits, or Optional State Supplementation.

Source: National Institute on Aging. Source: Florida AHCA.

Does Medicaid pay for assisted living in Florida? How do I apply?

It can, for people who qualify for Florida’s Statewide Medicaid Managed Care Long-Term Care program, which lists assisted living among its services. Start by calling your local Aging and Disability Resource Center for a screening; you may be placed on a waitlist based on that screening. When enrollment is available, the Department of Elder Affairs CARES program assesses medical eligibility (generally a nursing home level of care), and the Department of Children and Families determines financial eligibility. For general questions, AHCA points families to the statewide Elder Helpline at 1-800-963-5337 (1-800-96-ELDER).

Source: Florida AHCA, SMMC Long-Term Care FAQs. Source: Florida Dept of Elder Affairs, CARES.

What other ways can families pay (long-term care insurance, VA benefits, Optional State Supplementation)?

Besides private pay, Florida AHCA says to ask whether a facility accepts long-term care insurance, veterans benefits, or Optional State Supplementation (OSS), and to look for OSS-eligible facilities on FloridaHealthFinder. Some long-term care insurance policies help with assisted living, but coverage depends on the policy, so read it or call the insurer. Veterans or surviving spouses who already receive a VA pension and need help with daily activities, are housebound, or are in a nursing home because of disability-related loss of ability, may qualify for VA Aid and Attendance or a Housebound allowance; you apply through the VA.

Source: Florida AHCA. Source: U.S. Department of Veterans Affairs. Source: National Institute on Aging.

What happens if the family can no longer pay?

If residency is ending, Florida law generally requires at least 45 days’ written notice, except in certain emergency medical moves or when there is a pattern of harmful or offensive conduct. The contract must also include a refund policy for unused payment when a resident transfers, is discharged, or dies. If private payment is no longer possible, ask whether the community accepts Medicaid Long-Term Care and talk with the Aging and Disability Resource Center about screening. The notice of a non-emergency move must tell the resident they may contact the State Long-Term Care Ombudsman Program for help.

Source: Florida Statutes s. 429.28. Source: Florida Statutes s. 429.24. Source: Florida AHCA, SMMC Long-Term Care FAQs.

What paperwork or contract do I sign, and what should I review first?

Florida requires a written contract at or before admission, and you should receive a copy. The contract must list the services and accommodations, the rates or charges, at least 30 days’ written notice of a rate increase, and a refund policy if the resident transfers, is discharged, or dies. AHCA also says the contract should cover housing, optional services, and discharge and bed-hold policies. Take time to read those sections, including how unused days are prorated after the unit is vacated, before you sign.

Source: Florida Statutes s. 429.24. Source: Florida AHCA.

What happens if my loved one’s care needs change?

The administrator must keep deciding whether the person is still appropriate for that facility as needs change. An extended congregate care license is meant to help some residents age in place with more support, but a person who comes to need 24-hour nursing, or who is bedridden beyond the short limits in the law, generally cannot stay in assisted living (with limited exceptions, including some hospice care). For residents who receive ordered nursing services, a nurse must do at least monthly nursing assessments and note changes that may require a move. If a non-emergency relocation is needed, the resident is generally entitled to at least 45 days’ written notice.

Source: Florida Statutes s. 429.26. Source: Florida Statutes s. 429.07. Source: Florida AHCA. Source: Florida Statutes s. 429.28.

Could a resident be asked to leave, and what is the discharge policy?

Yes, but there are rules. A resident generally has a right to at least 45 days’ notice of a relocation or the end of residency, unless a physician certifies that an emergency move to a more skilled level of care is required, or the resident has a pattern of conduct that is harmful or offensive to other residents. The reasons must be put in writing, and the notice must include the statewide Long-Term Care Ombudsman phone number. The contract also has to describe the refund policy at discharge. To end a residency with no notice, the facility must show good cause in court.

Source: Florida Statutes s. 429.28. Source: Florida Statutes s. 429.24.

Do assisted living communities have a bed-hold policy if someone goes to the hospital?

Not automatically. AHCA says some facilities agree in writing to hold a bed, and the resident usually keeps paying until the hold ends or the facility gets written notice that the person will not return. Ask about the policy, any time limits, and the cost, and get the agreement in writing. FloridaHealthFinder can also show whether a facility reports a bed-hold policy.

Source: Florida AHCA.

Can staff help with or administer medications?

Yes, help with medications is a basic assisted living service in Florida, and some medications may be given by a nurse. A standard license also allows a facility to employ or contract with a nurse to administer medications and perform certain other tasks. Residents also have a right to assistance with medication management. Ask who is allowed to help versus administer medications in that community, and how that is documented.

Source: Florida AHCA. Source: Florida Statutes s. 429.07. Source: Florida Statutes s. 429.28.

Can a resident still use a home health agency while living in an ALF?

Often, yes. AHCA says a resident may contract with a licensed home health agency if they do not need 24-hour nursing, and they must still meet the facility’s residency rules. Home health does not turn an assisted living community into a nursing home. Ask the community how visiting home health services are coordinated.

Source: Florida AHCA.

Does the community help with medical appointments and transportation?

Florida law includes help making health care appointments, and providing or arranging transportation to those appointments, as part of a resident’s right to adequate and appropriate health care. AHCA’s consumer guide also lists transportation to health care among basic services, and it tells families to ask whether there is an extra fee. Confirm the community’s appointment and transportation practices — and any charges — before you sign.

Source: Florida Statutes s. 429.28. Source: Florida AHCA. Source: Florida Statutes s. 429.41.

Are special diets available?

It depends on the provider. AHCA says you should ask about special diets before you sign a contract. Florida’s assisted living rules are also required to address residents’ dietary needs, but that does not mean every community can meet every diet. Get the answer in writing if a special diet is important.

Source: Florida AHCA. Source: Florida Statutes s. 429.41.

What activities are offered?

Social and leisure activities are a basic assisted living service in Florida, and facilities are expected to provide or arrange them. Residents also have a right to regular exercise several times a week and to be outdoors at regular, frequent intervals except in bad weather. Activity calendars vary by community, so AHCA recommends asking to see the schedule and how residents get to activities.

Source: Florida AHCA. Source: Florida Statutes s. 429.41. Source: Florida Statutes s. 429.28.

Will my parent have a private room and bathroom, or a shared apartment?

Both exist. AHCA says assisted living may include private or shared rooms or apartments, and it lists “private versus shared room and bath” as a question to ask. A resident also has the right to share a room with a spouse if both live in the facility. Ask what unit types are available and what each one costs before you decide.

Source: Florida AHCA. Source: Florida Statutes s. 429.28.

Are pets allowed in assisted living communities?

Florida does not set one statewide pet rule for assisted living. AHCA’s consumer guide lists “Can I bring my pet(s)?” as a question families should ask, and FloridaHealthFinder may note whether a facility allows pets. Ask about the types and number of animals allowed, any deposits or fees, and whether rules change in certain parts of the community.

Source: Florida AHCA.

Can residents have overnight visitors? What are visiting policies?

FAQQuestions families often ask

Who qualifies for assisted living?

In Florida, assisted living is for adults who can no longer live independently and need help or supervision with daily activities or medications, or who need routine, simple nursing or mental health services. It is not the right setting if someone needs 24-hour nursing care. The facility’s owner or administrator decides whether a person can be admitted, based on a medical exam, the services the community can offer, and the limits of its license.

Source: Florida AHCA. Source: Florida Statutes s. 429.26.

How do I know if my parents need assisted living?

It is not always obvious, and a short phone call can hide how someone is really doing. The National Institute on Aging suggests spending time in the home and watching for signs such as trouble managing medications, changes around the house, significant weight change, mood changes, or memory and decision-making problems that go beyond ordinary forgetfulness. The best starting point is to ask your parent directly, then compare those signs with Florida’s picture of who is appropriate for assisted living — someone who needs help or supervision, but not 24-hour nursing.

Source: National Institute on Aging. Source: Florida AHCA.

Do they do an assessment before admission?

Yes. Florida law makes the owner or administrator responsible for deciding whether someone is appropriate for that facility, using a medical examination, the person’s needs and preferences, the services the facility can provide, and the limits of its license. Each resident must be examined by a licensed physician, physician assistant, or advanced practice registered nurse within 60 days before admission or within 30 days after admission. The examination is used to help decide whether admission — and later, continued residency — is appropriate.

Source: Florida Statutes s. 429.26.

What is the difference between assisted living and memory care?

Assisted living is a licensed residential setting that provides housing, meals, and personal help for people who need daily support but not 24-hour nursing. Memory care is specialized support for people with Alzheimer’s disease or another dementia; it may be offered inside an assisted living community, and not every assisted living community provides it. The Alzheimer’s Association notes that these services are not defined the same way everywhere, so you should ask what is actually offered, how staff are trained, and whether the area is secured. In Florida, a facility’s license can include “memory care services” as a category of care, along with a standard license and other specialty licenses.

Source: Florida AHCA. Source: Alzheimer's Association. Source: National Institute on Aging. Source: Florida Statutes s. 429.07.

What is the difference between assisted living and independent living?

Assisted living is for people who need help with daily care — such as bathing, dressing, medications, or supervision — but do not need the level of medical care in a nursing home. Independent living is generally for people who can manage on their own and want a maintenance-free home and social life, without that daily personal care. In Florida, a licensed assisted living facility is defined as a place that provides housing, meals, and one or more personal services for more than 24 hours.

Source: National Institute on Aging. Source: Florida Statutes s. 429.02.

What levels of care does assisted living provide?

Florida does not use a single numbered “level of care” for every community. Instead, AHCA issues licenses in categories such as standard, extended congregate care (ECC), limited nursing services, limited mental health, and memory care services. A standard license covers personal services such as housing, meals, and help with daily activities. An ECC license is meant to help residents age in place with more support, and a limited nursing services license allows certain nursing that is still not 24-hour nursing. Ask which license a community holds and what that means for the help they can give.

Source: Florida Statutes s. 429.07. Source: Florida AHCA.

Are nursing services provided in assisted living? Is there a nurse on staff?

It depends on the facility’s license. Nursing in assisted living is limited, and a need for 24-hour nursing generally means assisted living is no longer appropriate. Facilities with a standard license may employ or contract with a nurse to give medications and perform certain other tasks, and specialty licenses such as limited nursing services or extended congregate care allow additional nursing. Staffing and nurse availability vary, so ask whether a nurse is on site, on what schedule, and what they can and cannot do.

Source: Florida AHCA. Source: Florida Statutes s. 429.07.

Can someone with dementia stay in assisted living, or do they need memory care?

Some people with dementia can live in assisted living if the community can meet their needs and they do not require 24-hour nursing. Dementia services vary widely, and not every assisted living community offers specialized memory care. Florida’s consumer guide says to ask about special services, staff training, and equipment used to help prevent wandering. Visit and ask what the community can actually provide before you decide.

Source: Florida AHCA. Source: Alzheimer's Association.

Are there state rules for when someone must move from assisted living to memory care?

Florida does not have an automatic rule that someone must move from assisted living into a memory care unit at a certain stage of dementia. Whether a person can stay depends on whether that facility, under its license, can still meet their needs. In general, a Florida assisted living facility may not admit or keep someone who needs 24-hour nursing supervision or who is bedridden beyond short limits set in the statute (with some hospice and ECC exceptions). If needs change, ask the community what it can still provide and what the next step would be.

Source: Florida Statutes s. 429.26. Source: Florida AHCA.

Can couples with different care needs live together?

Florida law gives a resident the right to share a room with a spouse if both live in the facility. Each person is still assessed on their own, and both must remain appropriate for that community under its license and the services it can provide. If one spouse later needs more care than the facility can offer, the community may not be able to keep both people in the same setting. Ask how the community handles couples with different needs before you sign a contract.

Source: Florida Statutes s. 429.28. Source: Florida Statutes s. 429.26.

How much does assisted living cost?

There is no single price. Florida AHCA says fees are set by each provider and are not the same everywhere, and a community may charge a basic fee plus extra amounts for added services. The CareScout Cost of Care Survey 2025, collected from July through November 2025, lists a Florida median of $5,610 a month, or $67,320 a year, for an assisted living community. Local prices can be higher or lower, so ask each community for a current written rate.

Source: Florida AHCA. Source: CareScout Cost of Care Survey 2025.

What’s included in the monthly fee, and is pricing all-inclusive?

It is not always all-inclusive. Florida AHCA describes typical basic services such as housing, meals (including special diets when the facility offers them), personal care, help with medications, supervision, arranging health care, transportation to health care, health monitoring, and social activities — but a facility may also charge extra for additional services. The resident contract has to spell out the services and accommodations included and the rates or charges. Ask for a written list of what is in the monthly fee and what costs extra before you sign.

Source: Florida AHCA. Source: Florida Statutes s. 429.24.

Is there a move-in or community fee?

Florida does not set a standard move-in or community fee. Each facility sets its own charges, and the contract must list the rates or charges. Ask for a written explanation of any one-time fees, deposits, or advance payments, and how refunds work, before you sign.

Source: Florida AHCA. Source: Florida Statutes s. 429.24.

Can the monthly rate go up after move-in?

A new service or accommodation that was not previously charged does not require that 30-day notice. Read the contract so you know how and when rates can change.

Source: Florida Statutes s. 429.24. Source: Florida AHCA.

Source: National Institute on Aging. Source: Florida AHCA.

Does Medicaid pay for assisted living in Florida? How do I apply?

What other ways can families pay (long-term care insurance, VA benefits, Optional State Supplementation)?

Often, yes. AHCA says a resident may contract with a licensed home health agency if they do not need 24-hour nursing, and they must still meet the facility’s residency rules. Home health does not turn an assisted living community into a nursing home. Ask the community how visiting home health services are coordinated.

Source: Florida AHCA.

Does the community help with medical appointments and transportation?

Florida law includes help making health care appointments, and providing or arranging transportation to those appointments, as part of a resident’s right to adequate and appropriate health care. AHCA’s consumer guide also lists transportation to health care among basic services, and it tells families to ask whether there is an extra fee. Confirm the community’s appointment and transportation practices — and any charges — before you sign.

Source: Florida Statutes s. 429.28. Source: Florida AHCA. Source: Florida Statutes s. 429.41.

Are special diets available?

It depends on the provider. AHCA says you should ask about special diets before you sign a contract. Florida’s assisted living rules are also required to address residents’ dietary needs, but that does not mean every community can meet every diet. Get the answer in writing if a special diet is important.

Source: Florida AHCA. Source: Florida Statutes s. 429.41.

What activities are offered?

Social and leisure activities are a basic assisted living service in Florida, and facilities are expected to provide or arrange them. Residents also have a right to regular exercise several times a week and to be outdoors at regular, frequent intervals except in bad weather. Activity calendars vary by community, so AHCA recommends asking to see the schedule and how residents get to activities.

Source: Florida AHCA. Source: Florida Statutes s. 429.41. Source: Florida Statutes s. 429.28.

Will my parent have a private room and bathroom, or a shared apartment?

Both exist. AHCA says assisted living may include private or shared rooms or apartments, and it lists “private versus shared room and bath” as a question to ask. A resident also has the right to share a room with a spouse if both live in the facility. Ask what unit types are available and what each one costs before you decide.

Source: Florida AHCA. Source: Florida Statutes s. 429.28.

Are pets allowed in assisted living communities?

Florida does not set one statewide pet rule for assisted living. AHCA’s consumer guide lists “Can I bring my pet(s)?” as a question families should ask, and FloridaHealthFinder may note whether a facility allows pets. Ask about the types and number of animals allowed, any deposits or fees, and whether rules change in certain parts of the community.

Source: Florida AHCA.

Can residents have overnight visitors? What are visiting policies?

Florida law gives residents the right to visit with people they choose at least from 9 a.m. to 9 p.m., and the facility must extend visiting hours on request for caregivers, out-of-town guests, and similar situations. The statute does not spell out overnight stays, so ask the community what it allows and whether a guest can stay overnight. Get the visiting policy in writing so family members know what to expect.

Source: Florida Statutes s. 429.28.

What if my loved one struggles to adjust?

The National Institute on Aging notes that moving an older adult to an assisted living facility can be stressful and is a big change, and that it may take some time for everyone to adjust. Official suggestions include getting to know the staff before the move, being an advocate for the person’s needs and preferences, being supportive and listening, sharing your own feelings with a social worker or mental health professional, checking in regularly after the move, and building a relationship with staff. If something about care or rights is wrong, you can also contact the Florida Long-Term Care Ombudsman Program at 1-888-831-0404.

Source: National Institute on Aging. Source: Florida Long-Term Care Ombudsman Program.

How do I know if a facility is licensed in Florida?

A license from the Agency for Health Care Administration is required to operate an assisted living facility in Florida. AHCA’s consumer guide says you can ask to see the facility’s current license or look the community up on FloridaHealthFinder, which also has a comparison tool with license information, complaints, and other details. If a place cannot show a current AHCA license, keep looking.

Source: Florida Statutes s. 429.07. Source: Florida AHCA.
