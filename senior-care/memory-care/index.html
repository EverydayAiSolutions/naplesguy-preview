Memory Care

Alzheimer’s and dementia care in Naples

Worried about Mom or Dad’s memory? Start here.

Families usually land here after a scare — a missed dose, a fall, or a parent who got lost. Here is what memory care actually is, what it costs, and what to watch for on a tour, in plain words.

Signs it may be time

  • Memory loss that gets in the way of daily life — medications, bills, the stove
  • Getting lost, wandering, or mixing up day and night
  • No longer safe at home, even with help in the house

See the 10 early signs →

What memory care is

  • A secured home designed so people with dementia can walk freely without slipping out
  • Staff trained in dementia, on duty around the clock — including overnight
  • A steady routine of meals, music and activities that actually take place

How it differs from assisted living

  • Assisted living helps with bathing, dressing and medications
  • Memory care adds secured exits, closer supervision, and staff trained for confusion and sundowning
  • In the earlier stages, some people do well in regular assisted living

Can she stay in assisted living? →

Cost and how families pay

  • Usually costs more than assisted living, and the monthly fee can rise as needs grow
  • Medicare does not pay for long-term memory care
  • Florida Medicaid, VA Aid and Attendance, or long-term care insurance may help if you qualify

Who pays, in plain terms →

What to look for on a tour

  • Ask how many staff are on the night shift — days and nights are not the same
  • Doors should be secured without feeling like a lockup. Ask how they handle sundowning
  • Familiar photos in the rooms, and an activity calendar you can see happening, not just posted

Three simple steps

  1. See the doctorGet a check-up and a clear diagnosis. A medication, depression, a vitamin deficiency or a thyroid problem can look like dementia.
  2. Look at the optionsHelp at home, adult day, assisted living or memory care. Compare them.

Quick answers

What are the 10 early signs of Alzheimer’s?

The Alzheimer’s Association lists these signs:

  • Memory loss that disrupts daily life
  • Trouble planning or solving problems
  • Difficulty finishing familiar tasks
  • Confusion with time or place
  • Trouble understanding images and distances
  • New problems with words when speaking or writing
  • Misplacing things and not being able to retrace steps
  • Poor judgment
  • Pulling away from work or social activities
  • Changes in mood and personality

Sometimes forgetting a name and remembering it later is a normal part of aging. If you notice these signs, see a doctor. The Association’s 24/7 Helpline is 800-272-3900.

Can someone with dementia stay in regular assisted living?

Often yes, in the earlier stages. Florida does not require a move to memory care at a set stage of dementia. It depends on whether that community, under its license, can still meet the person’s needs.

A Florida assisted living community generally cannot keep someone who needs 24-hour nursing care. Families usually look at memory care when wandering starts, when someone tries to leave, or when they need more supervision than assisted living can give. More on assisted living.

Does Florida license memory care?

Florida does not have a separate memory care license in use today. Memory care is usually a unit or program inside a licensed assisted living community.

If a community advertises special care for Alzheimer’s, Florida requires an awake staff member on duty around the clock (very small communities may use other safety monitoring), activities designed for people with memory loss, a safe setting, and extra dementia training for staff.

On the official state profile, look at the license type. An Extended Congregate Care license means the community can give more support as needs grow, so a later move may not come as soon. Look up any community’s license and inspection reports on FloridaHealthFinder.

Who pays for memory care?

Most families pay privately with income, savings or the sale of a home. Memory care usually costs more than assisted living because it has more staff and security. For comparison, typical assisted living in Florida runs about $5,610 a month. Ask each community for its memory care rate in writing, and how that rate can change.

  • Medicare does not pay for long-term care like memory care. It does cover doctor visits, hospital stays and hospice.
  • Florida Medicaid’s long-term care program can cover assisted living services for people who qualify. Screening is done by phone through the Elder Helpline at 1-800-963-5337. There is often a waitlist, and not everyone who needs memory care will qualify.
  • VA Aid and Attendance may help veterans and surviving spouses who qualify for a VA pension and need help with daily activities or supervision. Start at VA.gov.
  • Long-term care insurance may help, depending on the policy. Read the policy before you tour, and ask the community how claims are billed.

More on Paying for Care.

Why does the monthly fee go up?

Many communities charge a room rate plus a care-level fee. As the person needs more help with bathing, dressing, walking or incontinence, that fee can climb. Some also add charges for medication help, supplies or one-on-one supervision.

Ask for the written list of care levels, what each one includes, and what triggers a jump. Get the monthly rate, the extras, and how often rates can change in the contract before you sign. Florida requires the resident contract to spell out the services and the charges.

What should we ask on a tour?

Go more than once. Visit at a meal, and again in the late afternoon, when many people with dementia become restless. Watch the room, not just the brochure.

  • How many direct-care staff are on this unit during the day, at night, and on weekends? Ask for the written schedule. Florida requires the community to share the daily direct-care work schedule if you ask.
  • What dementia training has the staff on this unit completed, and when was the last refresher? Communities that advertise specialized Alzheimer’s care must complete extra hours, plus training every year.
  • How are exits secured? Residents should be able to walk freely, including in a safe courtyard, without the place feeling locked down.
  • What do you do when someone becomes anxious or agitated between 4 and 8 p.m.? Good programs keep evenings calmer and well lit, stick to a routine, offer something soothing, and do not use restraints.
  • May we bring familiar photos, bedding and a chair so the room feels like home?
  • Can we see today’s activity calendar — and is that activity happening right now? What is offered in the evenings and on weekends?
  • What is the monthly rate, what is included, and how do care-level fees go up as needs change?
  • If needs grow, can she stay? Does the community have an Extended Congregate Care license? When would she have to move?
  • What happens if the money runs out? Does the community accept Medicaid?
Who do we call if something seems wrong?

Call the Florida Long-Term Care Ombudsman Program at 1-888-831-0404. You can also check inspection reports and file a complaint through FloridaHealthFinder.
