01Memory care in Florida

Quick answer
  • Memory care is specialized support for people with Alzheimer’s disease or another dementia. It may be offered inside an assisted living community. Alzheimer's Association.
  • These services are not defined the same way everywhere, so ask what is actually offered, how staff are trained, and whether the area is secured. Alzheimer's Association.
  • In Florida, a facility’s license can include memory care services as a category of care. Florida Statutes s. 429.07.
  • A neutral list of state-licensed facilities in Naples and nearby, including those with a state Extended Congregate Care or Specialized Alzheimer’s Services license, is on the Senior Living Directory.

02Memory care in Naples, and in Florida

If you are looking at memory care in Naples, start with what the community is actually licensed and staffed to provide. Some people with dementia can live in assisted living if the community can meet their needs and they do not require 24-hour nursing. Florida does not automatically require a move into a memory care unit at a certain stage of dementia. Whether a person can stay depends on whether that facility, under its license, can still meet their needs. In general, a Florida assisted living facility may not admit or keep someone who needs 24-hour nursing supervision or who is bedridden beyond short limits set in the statute (with some hospice and ECC exceptions).

What this means for you. The label “memory care” is not a substitute for reading the license and asking what staff actually do, day and night, for someone who may wander or need more help.

06Memory care vs. assisted living

Assisted living is a licensed residential setting that provides housing, meals, and personal help for people who need daily support but not 24-hour nursing. Memory care is specialized support for people with Alzheimer’s disease or another dementia; it may be offered inside an assisted living community. The Alzheimer’s Association notes that these services are not defined the same way everywhere, so you should ask what is actually offered, how staff are trained, and whether the area is secured. In Florida, a facility’s license can include “memory care services” as a category of care, along with a standard license and other specialty licenses.

Some people with dementia can live in assisted living if the community can meet their needs and they do not require 24-hour nursing. Florida’s consumer guide says to ask about special services, staff training, and equipment used to help prevent wandering.

07Signs it may be time

The National Institute on Aging suggests watching for trouble managing medications, changes around the house, significant weight change, mood changes, or memory and decision-making problems that go beyond ordinary forgetfulness. Those signs can be a reason to look more closely at the help someone needs, including whether specialized memory support would fit. 08Florida rules

Florida does not have an automatic rule that someone must move from assisted living into a memory care unit at a certain stage of dementia. Whether a person can stay depends on whether that facility, under its license, can still meet their needs. In general, a Florida assisted living facility may not admit or keep someone who needs 24-hour nursing supervision or who is bedridden beyond short limits set in the statute (with some hospice and ECC exceptions). If needs change, ask the community what it can still provide and what the next step would be.

Source: Florida Statutes s. 429.26. Source: Florida AHCA.

09A short checklist

A short checklist
  • Ask whether the community’s license includes memory care services.
  • Ask what is actually offered, how staff are trained, and whether the area is secured.
  • Ask about equipment or practices used to help prevent wandering.
  • Ask what happens if care needs change, and whether 24-hour nursing would mean a move.

10Is it time? Official signs and a checklist

Quick answer
  • The National Institute on Aging suggests spending time in the home and watching for certain signs that an older adult may need more help. National Institute on Aging.
  • Those signs can include trouble managing medications, changes around the house, significant weight change, mood changes, or memory and decision-making problems beyond ordinary forgetfulness.

11Official signs that more help may be needed

It is not always obvious, and a short phone call can hide how someone is really doing. The National Institute on Aging suggests spending time in the home and watching for signs such as trouble managing medications, changes around the house, significant weight change, mood changes, or memory and decision-making problems that go beyond ordinary forgetfulness. The best starting point is to ask your parent or loved one directly, then compare those signs with official descriptions of each type of care.

What this means for you. This list is a conversation starter, not a diagnosis and not a decision about assisted living. If several signs are present, use the checklist below, then look at Which care fits? or call the Elder Helpline at 1-800-963-5337.

14Florida ECC and Specialized Alzheimer’s licenses

Extended Congregate Care (ECC). A specialty license that lets some assisted living facilities provide a higher level of care, so a resident may be able to stay as needs increase. Florida does not issue a separate “memory care” license.
