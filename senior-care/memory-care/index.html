02What memory care is, and how it differs from assisted living

Memory care is specialized support for people with Alzheimer’s disease or another dementia. It may be offered inside an assisted living community, and not every assisted living community provides it. The Alzheimer’s Association notes that these services are not defined the same way everywhere.

Assisted living in Florida is a licensed setting that provides housing, meals, and personal help for people who need daily support but not 24-hour nursing. Some people with dementia can live in assisted living if the community can meet their needs and they do not require 24-hour nursing. Florida does not automatically require a move into a memory care unit at a certain stage of dementia. Whether a person can stay depends on whether that facility, under its license, can still meet their needs. In general, a Florida assisted living facility may not admit or keep someone who needs 24-hour nursing supervision or who is bedridden beyond short limits set in the statute (with some hospice and Extended Congregate Care exceptions).

A fuller comparison is on Memory Care vs. Assisted Living.

03Official signs that more help may be needed

These lists are a starting point for a conversation with the older adult and a doctor. They are not a diagnosis, and they do not, by themselves, decide whether memory care is the right setting.

The National Institute on Aging suggests spending time in the home and watching for signs such as trouble managing medications, changes around the house, significant weight change, mood changes, or memory and decision-making problems that go beyond ordinary forgetfulness.

The Alzheimer’s Association publishes 10 early signs and symptoms of Alzheimer’s and other dementia, and it asks families not to ignore them and to schedule an appointment with a doctor. The Association’s 10 signs are: memory loss that disrupts daily life; challenges in planning or solving problems; difficulty completing familiar tasks; confusion with time or place; trouble understanding visual images and spatial relationships; new problems with words in speaking or writing; misplacing things and losing the ability to retrace steps; decreased or poor judgment; withdrawal from work or social activities; and changes in mood and personality. The Association contrasts those signs with typical age-related changes, such as sometimes forgetting names or appointments and remembering them later. The full comparison is on the Association’s official page. The Association’s 24/7 Helpline is 800-272-3900.

04The Alzheimer’s Support Network in Naples

The Alzheimer’s Support Network is a Naples organization whose stated purpose is to enrich the lives of people living with Alzheimer’s and other forms of neurodegeneration and to ease the lives of their care partners. Its office is at 1421 Pine Ridge Road, Suite 100, Naples, FL 34109. Official office hours are Monday through Friday, 9 a.m. to 4 p.m. The published phone number is 239-262-8388, and families can also write to help@support.network or info@support.network. Details are on the Alzheimer's Support Network. A fuller guide to their support groups, programs, and current official calendar is on this site: Alzheimer’s Support Network in Naples.

On its programs and care-guiding pages, the Network describes family consultants (care guides) who meet with families in person, by phone, Zoom, or email. It likes to schedule a private meeting with new families to learn about the situation. In-person meetings take place in Collier County; the Network also meets with families elsewhere by Zoom. Its care-guiding page states that it does not perform cognitive assessments, memory screenings, or formal interviews. It follows a social rather than medical model and invites families to share what is happening.

Official programs described on its site include:

  • Care partner support groups, in person and on Zoom, including general groups and groups for particular situations. The programs page says the Network has over 30 support groups each month, including disease-specific groups such as Lewy body, frontotemporal, and vascular dementia groups, and groups for audiences such as adult children.
  • Educational events, including some that are recorded so families can watch later.
  • Private family meetings, and help connecting families with outside support such as home health, respite, and other community resources.
  • The Club Room, also called the Brain Fitness Center, offered during general support groups for people living with brain change. The support groups page says this is not a drop-off service: care partners must remain in the building.
  • Kindred Spirits, a discussion group for people in the early stage of Alzheimer’s or another form of cognitive impairment.
  • Ladies and Gentlemen’s Clubs, which the Network describes as engagement for people living with brain change and a break for care partners. Registration is required; details are on the engagement page.
  • Community outings listed on the engagement page, such as botanical garden sensory tours.
  • The Wanderer’s Identification Program, offered with the Collier County Sheriff’s Office, which collects information and a current photograph and provides an identification bracelet or necklace so a person who wanders can be identified and returned home. An application and a caregiver release are required. Details are on the official program page.

These are the Network’s programs, described from its official pages. They are a local resource for families, not a substitute for a medical evaluation or a Florida license check of a community. Start with the programs page, the support groups page, care guiding, engagement programs, and the Wanderer’s ID Program.

05Teepa Snow’s Positive Approach to Care

Teepa Snow’s Positive Approach to Care is a dementia education approach published on teepasnow.com. Official PAC materials should be read on that site. What follows is only what those official pages say.

PAC describes dementia as an umbrella term that covers many types, causes, and forms of brain change — official PAC materials say there are over 120. It notes that each person and each dementia may progress differently. Teepa’s GEMS model is described as an ability-based progression model that shows some of the changes in the brain and in skills that can be expected. Official Positive Approach to Care materials name six GEMS states: Sapphire, Diamond, Emerald, Amber, Ruby, and Pearl. PAC’s public teaching emphasizes looking at current abilities and using what remains, rather than focusing only on what has been lost.

Positive Approach to Care skills are described as practical skills “to get in and get connected before getting the task done.” Official materials say a care partner should know their agenda, but not show their agenda. The Positive Physical Approach (PPA) is described as a dynamic assessment of the other person’s willingness, comfort, and ability to engage and interact. PPA “uses as many retained skills as possible in order to do with, not to” when caring for someone living with dementia.

An official PAC explanation of the Positive Physical Approach includes coming into the person’s visual range (about six feet away) and pausing; greeting the person; offering a hand in a handshake position; approaching slowly from the front if the person extends a hand; moving from a handshake into the Hand-under-Hand support position; and getting at or below eye level without leaning in. Hand-under-Hand is an official PAC support position used as part of that approach. The steps are published on PAC’s Visual, Verbal, then Touch page. Families who want to learn the skills should use official PAC materials, not this summary alone.

This approach is not a diagnosis, and it does not replace a medical exam or a Florida license check of a community.

Source: Positive Approach to Care, About. Source: Positive Approach to Care, Visual, Verbal, then Touch.

06Questions to ask on a tour

The Alzheimer’s Association notes that memory care services are not defined the same way everywhere, so families should ask what is actually offered, how staff are trained, and whether the area is secured. Florida’s official assisted living consumer guide says to ask about special services, staff training, and equipment used to help prevent wandering. Visit and ask what the community can actually provide before deciding. Write down the answers and compare them with the AHCA license on FloridaHealthFinder.

07Official lookups and who to call

Families can browse the Senior Living Directory for a neutral list of state-licensed places in Naples and nearby, then open each official FloridaHealthFinder profile from the card. The directory can be filtered for communities whose official profile includes a memory-care designation, including a state Extended Congregate Care license or a Specialized Alzheimer’s Services license.

Look the community up on FloridaHealthFinder for license and inspection information. If something about care or rights is wrong, contact the Florida Long-Term Care Ombudsman Program at 1-888-831-0404, or file a complaint with AHCA. For a broader starting point in Collier County, call the Elder Helpline at 1-800-963-5337, or the Collier listing 239-652-6901. The Alzheimer’s Association Helpline is 800-272-3900. The Alzheimer’s Support Network in Naples can be reached at 239-262-8388.

08Florida ECC and Specialized Alzheimer’s licenses

Extended Congregate Care (ECC). A specialty license that lets some assisted living facilities provide a higher level of care, so a resident may be able to stay as needs increase. Florida does not issue a separate “memory care” license. The Memory care filter on the Senior Living Directory shows facilities with a state ECC license, ECC beds, or a Specialized Alzheimer’s Services license.

Does Medicare pay for memory care?

Medicare does not pay the monthly assisted living or memory care fee. People usually pay privately, though Florida Medicaid Long-Term Care may help people who qualify, and some long-term care insurance policies or VA Aid and Attendance benefits may apply. The Naples senior living cost guide explains official CareScout, Medicaid, and VA figures, and marks unpublished community prices as call for pricing.
