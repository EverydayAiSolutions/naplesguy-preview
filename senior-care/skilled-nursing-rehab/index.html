01Skilled nursing and rehab

Quick answer
  • Rehab in a skilled nursing facility is short-term skilled care that Medicare Part A may cover when you qualify. Medicare generally does not cover long-term custodial nursing home stays. Medicare.gov, skilled nursing facility care.
  • For Original Medicare in 2026, you pay $0 per day for days 1–20 after the Part A deductible, $217 per day for days 21–100, and all costs after day 100 in a benefit period. Medicare.gov, 2026 costs.
  • Original Medicare usually requires a qualifying three-day inpatient hospital stay. Observation time, even overnight, does not count. Medicare.gov, skilled nursing facility care.

02What Medicare covers, and what it does not

Rehab in a skilled nursing facility is short-term, skilled care — for example, physical therapy after a joint replacement, or skilled nursing to recover from an illness — that Medicare Part A may cover when you qualify. Long-term or custodial nursing home care is ongoing help with daily activities such as bathing, dressing, or eating. Medicare generally does not cover long-term nursing home stays unless you are receiving covered skilled care.

Original Medicare usually requires a qualifying three-day inpatient hospital stay. Observation time, even overnight, does not count toward that stay. Count the admission day but not the discharge day. You generally must enter the SNF within 30 days of leaving the hospital, and the facility must be Medicare-certified. Covered days last only while you still need skilled care, up to 100 days in a benefit period. For Original Medicare in 2026, Medicare.gov lists $0 per day for days 1–20 after the Part A deductible, $217 per day for days 21–100, and all costs after day 100. You do not have to pay the $1,736 Part A deductible again for the SNF stay if you already paid it for a hospital stay in the same benefit period. Medicare Advantage plans may charge different copays, including in the first 20 days, so check your plan.

What this means for you. Ask the hospital, before discharge, whether the stay is inpatient or observation, whether Medicare is expected to cover the SNF, and whether a bed is available in a Medicare-certified facility. A rushed discharge is easier if those points are written down.

A hospital-discharge checklist is on Hospital Discharge.

03How to choose a nursing home in Naples

A neutral list of state-licensed nursing homes and hospital skilled nursing units in Naples and nearby, with each facility’s official FloridaHealthFinder profile and Medicare star rating, is on the Senior Living Directory.

If you are choosing a nursing home or rehab facility in Naples after a hospital stay, start with official tools. If you are in the hospital, ask the discharge planner or social worker for a list of local skilled nursing facilities; they may also help you find an available bed. Medicare’s Care Compare lets you find and compare Medicare-certified nursing homes. CMS’s Five-Star Quality Rating System, shown on Care Compare, gives each nursing home a rating of 1 to 5 stars; CMS says 5 stars means much above average quality and 1 star means quality much below average. Take Medicare’s official nursing home visit checklist with you.

The National Institute on Aging suggests asking about costs and special needs, visiting more than once, including at mealtime, and looking for Medicare and Medicaid certification, state licensing, accessibility, residents who look well cared for, respectful staff, and a clean, well-maintained facility. Read the contract carefully. FloridaHealthFinder can show Florida license and complaint information. If you have a concern about care or rights, contact the Florida Long-Term Care Ombudsman Program at 1-888-831-0404. If you have a Medicare Advantage plan, ask whether the SNF is in the plan’s network and whether you must notify the plan before admission.

What this means for you. When the hospital is pushing for a same-day decision, use Care Compare and the official checklist anyway, even if the first visit is short. You can ask about a later transfer if the first available bed is not a good fit.

04Questions families often ask

What is the difference between rehab in a skilled nursing facility and long-term nursing home care?

Rehab in a skilled nursing facility is short-term, skilled care — for example, physical therapy after a joint replacement, or skilled nursing to recover from an illness — that Medicare Part A may cover when you qualify. Long-term or custodial nursing home care is ongoing help with daily activities such as bathing, dressing, or eating. Medicare generally does not cover long-term nursing home stays unless you are receiving covered skilled care.

Source: Medicare.gov, skilled nursing facility care. Source: Medicare.gov, nursing home care. Source: Medicare, Your Guide to Choosing a Nursing Home.

Does Medicare cover long-term or custodial nursing home care?

Generally, no. Medicare does not pay for custodial care, which is non-skilled personal help with daily activities, and it generally does not cover long-term nursing home stays. Even when Medicare is not paying for the nursing home stay itself, you still need Medicare for hospital care, doctor services, and other medical coverage. If you need long-term care, ask about Medicaid, private payment, or other options.

Source: Medicare.gov, nursing home care. Source: Medicare.gov, how to pay for nursing home care.

What services does Medicare cover in a SNF (therapy, nursing, room, meals, medications)?

When you qualify, Medicare-covered SNF care includes a semi-private room, meals, skilled nursing care, physical therapy, occupational therapy, and speech-language pathology services when they are needed to meet your health goal, plus medical social services, medications, medical supplies, and ambulance transportation when other transportation would endanger your health. Examples of skilled care include physical therapy or intravenous medications that must be given by a therapist or registered nurse. You must get this care in a Medicare-certified SNF, from or under the supervision of skilled nursing or therapy staff.

Source: Medicare.gov, skilled nursing facility care. Source: Medicare, Coverage of Skilled Nursing Facility Care.

Who qualifies for Medicare coverage in a skilled nursing facility?

For Original Medicare, you generally need a qualifying inpatient hospital stay, you must enter the SNF within a short time (generally 30 days) of leaving the hospital, and you must need daily skilled nursing or therapy related to the condition treated in the hospital (or another condition treated during that stay). The care has to be given in a Medicare-certified skilled nursing facility. If you have a Medicare Advantage plan, costs and some rules can differ, so check with your plan.

Source: Medicare.gov, skilled nursing facility care. Source: Medicare, Coverage of Skilled Nursing Facility Care.

What is the 3-day qualifying inpatient hospital stay?

For Original Medicare, you generally must be admitted as an inpatient for at least three days in a row, counting the admission day but not the discharge day, before Medicare will cover a SNF stay. Time in the emergency room or under observation does not count toward those three days. Always ask your doctor or hospital staff whether the stay is inpatient and whether Medicare is expected to cover the SNF care.

Source: Medicare.gov, skilled nursing facility care.

Does time in the emergency room or under observation count toward the 3-day stay?

No. Time you spend in the emergency room or under observation, even overnight, does not count toward the three-day inpatient stay Original Medicare requires for SNF coverage. You are considered an outpatient during observation, and those days are not part of a qualifying inpatient stay. If you do not have a qualifying inpatient stay, ask about other options, such as home health care, and whether any other program might help.

Source: Medicare.gov, skilled nursing facility care.

Do I have to enter the SNF within 30 days of leaving the hospital?

Generally, yes. Medicare says you must enter the SNF within a short time — generally 30 days — after leaving the hospital, and you must need skilled services related to your hospital stay. If you later leave the SNF and return to the same or another SNF within 30 days, you usually do not need another three-day hospital stay to get additional SNF benefits, as long as you still meet the other coverage rules.

Source: Medicare.gov, skilled nursing facility care. Source: Medicare, Coverage of Skilled Nursing Facility Care.

Do Medicare Advantage plans follow the same 3-day rule?

Not always. Medicare.gov notes that some Medicare Advantage plans may waive the three-day minimum inpatient hospital stay. Costs and coverage can also differ from Original Medicare, including possible copays in the first 20 days, so contact your plan and ask whether it has a “Skilled Nursing Facility 3-Day Rule Waiver.” Your doctor or hospital staff can help you check before you move to a SNF.

Source: Medicare.gov, skilled nursing facility care.

Can I appeal if the hospital changed my status from inpatient to observation?

In some cases, yes. Medicare.gov says that if your hospital status was changed to outpatient observation, you may be able to appeal a related denial of Part A hospital or SNF coverage for stays going back to January 2009. If the appeal is approved, Part A may cover the hospital and SNF services that were denied because of the status change. Follow Medicare’s instructions for filing that type of appeal, and ask the hospital for help if you are unsure where to start.

Source: Medicare.gov, skilled nursing facility care.

How many days will Medicare cover in a SNF?

Original Medicare covers up to 100 days of skilled nursing facility care in each benefit period, as long as you remain eligible and still need covered skilled care. Coverage can end sooner if you no longer need daily skilled nursing or therapy. After day 100 in a benefit period, you pay all of the SNF costs.

Source: Medicare.gov, skilled nursing facility care. Source: Medicare.gov, 2026 costs. Source: Medicare, Coverage of Skilled Nursing Facility Care.

What do I pay for days 1–20, days 21–100, and after day 100?

For Original Medicare in 2026, you pay $0 per day for days 1–20 after the Part A deductible, $217 per day for days 21–100, and all costs after day 100 in a benefit period. You do not have to pay the $1,736 Part A deductible again for the SNF stay if you already paid it for a hospital stay in the same benefit period. Medicare Advantage plans may charge different copays, including in the first 20 days, so check your plan.

Source: Medicare.gov, skilled nursing facility care. Source: Medicare.gov, 2026 costs.

What is a Medicare benefit period, and when does a new one start?

A benefit period is how Original Medicare measures your use of inpatient hospital and SNF care. It begins the day you are admitted as an inpatient to a hospital or SNF, and it ends when you have not received any inpatient hospital care or skilled care in a SNF for 60 days in a row. If you are admitted again after that, a new benefit period starts, you may again have up to 100 SNF days, and you pay the Part A deductible for the new period. There is no limit on the number of benefit periods.

Source: Medicare.gov, skilled nursing facility care. Source: Medicare.gov, 2026 costs.

Can Medicare coverage end before 100 days if skilled care is no longer needed?

Yes. The 100-day maximum applies only while you still qualify, and coverage can end earlier when you no longer need daily skilled nursing or therapy. If you disagree with a discharge or a decision that skilled care is no longer needed, you can appeal. Ask the facility to explain the decision in writing and how to file the appeal.

Source: Medicare.gov, skilled nursing facility care. Source: Medicare, Coverage of Skilled Nursing Facility Care.

If I leave the SNF or go back to the hospital, can I return and still have Medicare days left?

It depends on how long you are away and whether you still need skilled care. If your break in SNF care lasts less than 30 days, you generally do not need a new three-day hospital stay, and you can use any unused SNF days left in the same benefit period. If the break lasts more than 30 days, you generally need a new three-day inpatient hospital stay to qualify for more SNF coverage. If you go 60 days in a row without inpatient hospital care or skilled SNF care, the benefit period ends, and a new one can start with up to 100 days if you qualify again.

Source: Medicare.gov, skilled nursing facility care. Source: Medicare, Coverage of Skilled Nursing Facility Care.

How does hospital discharge to a SNF work, and who helps choose the facility?

If you are in the hospital, ask the discharge planner or social worker for a list of local skilled nursing facilities; they may also help you find an available bed. If the hospital has its own SNF and a bed is open, you may choose to stay there; if not, you will need a bed at another Medicare-certified facility. Medicare also points families to Care Compare at Medicare.gov to look up and compare nursing homes, including rehab services. Always ask your doctor or hospital staff whether Medicare is expected to cover the SNF stay.

Source: Medicare, Coverage of Skilled Nursing Facility Care. Source: Medicare.gov, skilled nursing facility care. Source: Medicare, Your Guide to Choosing a Nursing Home.

Does the SNF have to be Medicare-certified, and what if it is the hospital’s own SNF?

Yes. For Medicare to cover the stay, you must receive the skilled care in a Medicare-certified SNF. If the hospital you are in has its own SNF and a bed is available, you may choose to stay there; otherwise you will need an available bed at a separate Medicare-certified facility. With Original Medicare you can go to any Medicare-certified SNF that has a bed; a Medicare Advantage plan may require or encourage you to use a SNF in the plan’s network, so call the plan before you are admitted.

Source: Medicare.gov, skilled nursing facility care. Source: Medicare, Coverage of Skilled Nursing Facility Care.

What questions should I ask before accepting a SNF bed (insurance, therapy, expected length of stay)?

Start by confirming that the facility is Medicare-certified, that it offers the skilled care you need, and that a bed is available, and ask your doctor or hospital staff whether Medicare will cover the stay. If you have a Medicare Advantage plan, ask whether the SNF is in the plan’s network and whether you must notify the plan before admission. Official Medicare checklists also suggest asking what rehab services are provided, whether the facility accepts Medicare and Medicaid, and which services are included versus billed separately. Covered days last only while you still need skilled care, up to 100 days in a benefit period, so ask the care team how they will review that with you.

Source: Medicare, Coverage of Skilled Nursing Facility Care. Source: Medicare, Your Guide to Choosing a Nursing Home. Source: Medicare.gov, skilled nursing facility care.

What happens after rehab — can the person go home, or might they need long-term care?

It depends on how the person is doing when skilled care ends. Some people go home, sometimes with other services such as home health; others may still need help with daily activities, which is custodial or long-term care that Medicare generally does not cover. If long-term nursing home care is needed, Medicare says most people begin by paying privately, and Medicaid may help if the person qualifies. Start that conversation with the SNF’s care team before discharge so you are not surprised when skilled coverage ends.

Source: Medicare.gov, nursing home care. Source: Medicare.gov, how to pay for nursing home care. Source: Medicare, Coverage of Skilled Nursing Facility Care.

If Medicare will not cover the stay, how can families pay for nursing home care (Medicaid, VA, private pay)?

Medicare says most people who enter a nursing home start by paying out of pocket, and Medicare generally does not cover long-term nursing home care. Other possible ways to pay include Medicaid (if the person meets the state’s rules; most but not all nursing homes accept Medicaid), long-term care insurance if the policy covers the care, and VA benefits for some Veterans or survivors, including Aid and Attendance when they qualify. If you have a policy or VA pension, call the insurer or the VA to see what applies to this stay.

Source: Medicare.gov, how to pay for nursing home care. Source: Medicare.gov, nursing home care. Source: U.S. Department of Veterans Affairs.

In Florida, what is the CARES screening for nursing home or Medicaid long-term care?

CARES is the Department of Elder Affairs Comprehensive Assessment and Review for Long-Term Care Services program. It determines medical eligibility — generally a nursing home level of care — for Medicaid nursing facility services and for home- and community-based services through programs such as Statewide Medicaid Managed Care Long-Term Care. A registered nurse or assessor usually does an in-person assessment at no cost to the person, and financial eligibility is decided separately by the Department of Children and Families. To get started on the LTC program, call your local Aging and Disability Resource Center for a screening, or the Elder Helpline at 1-800-963-5337.

Source: Florida Dept of Elder Affairs, CARES. Source: Florida AHCA, SMMC Long-Term Care FAQs.

06Hospital-discharge rush checklist

When discharge is happening quickly, these official Medicare points are the ones to confirm with the hospital team:

  • Ask whether the hospital stay is inpatient or observation. Observation time, even overnight, does not count toward the three-day inpatient stay Original Medicare usually requires for skilled nursing coverage.
  • Count the admission day but not the discharge day when you ask whether there has been a qualifying three-day inpatient stay.
  • Ask whether Medicare is expected to cover a skilled nursing facility stay, and whether the facility is Medicare-certified.
  • Ask about the timing: you generally must enter the SNF within 30 days of leaving the hospital.
  • If hospital status was changed to outpatient observation, ask about appealing a related denial of Part A hospital or SNF coverage.
  • If you have a Medicare Advantage plan, ask whether it waives the three-day rule and what copays apply.
  • For Original Medicare in 2026, Medicare.gov lists $0 per day for days 1–20 after the Part A deductible, $217 per day for days 21–100, and all costs after day 100 in a benefit period. Check your plan if you have Medicare Advantage.

Source: Medicare.gov, skilled nursing facility care. Source: Medicare.gov, 2026 costs.

It determines medical eligibility — generally a nursing home level of care — for Medicaid nursing facility services and for home- and community-based services through programs such as Statewide Medicaid Managed Care Long-Term Care. A registered nurse or assessor usually does an in-person assessment at no cost to the person, and financial eligibility is decided separately by the Department of Children and Families. To get started on the LTC program, call your local Aging and Disability Resource Center for a screening, or the Elder Helpline at 1-800-963-5337.

Source: Florida Dept of Elder Affairs, CARES. Source: Florida AHCA, SMMC Long-Term Care FAQs.
